  <h1>Seeing what plants need: AI vision and tiered automation for growers</h1>
  <p class="p-sub">What a photo can and cannot tell, how to predict needs before stress shows, and three fields read from orbit in 2026</p>
  <p class="meta">Hortus Research, lastfomo · Data to 25 October 2026</p>
</header>

<section class="abstract" aria-label="Abstract">
  <p><b>Background</b>Phone cameras and AI image models promise cheap plant diagnosis, and automation promises to run a growing space on its own. Lab accuracy has not carried over to the field, and automation is usually sold as a package rather than as steps a grower can check.</p>
  <p><b>Aim</b>To set out what image analytics can and cannot tell a grower, to build a transparent analyser and a needs predictor that run in the browser, and to stage automation in tiers, tested against 2026 satellite and weather data for three real fields.</p>
  <p><b>Methods</b>A review of evidence on image-based diagnosis outside the lab; a colour-index analyser (Excess Green with an Otsu threshold, VARI, and hue rules inside the plant outline) with an optional multimodal second opinion; a needs model built on FAO-56 Penman–Monteith reference evapotranspiration, crop coefficients, daily light integral, vapour pressure deficit and the Hutton criteria; five automation tiers mapped to controllers; and Sentinel-2 time series with NASA POWER weather for 6 × 6 km windows in Kansas (USA), Mkushi (Zambia) and West Java (Indonesia).</p>
  <p><b>Results</b>Published models drop from 99.35% accuracy on lab images to 31.4% on photos taken elsewhere, and eight background pixels predict lab labels with 49% accuracy; a general multimodal model reached 56.9% zero-shot on apple leaves. In Kansas, NDVI curves separated five crop calendars; irrigated maize (46% of the window) used 735–936 mm of water against 436 mm of rain in a summer as hot as 2012, implying 520–714 mm of irrigation. Mkushi’s pivots carried winter wheat through a season with 2.6 mm of rain, needing 534–644 mm. In West Java the share of rice land reaching green canopy in the dry season fell from 91% in 2025 to 62% in 2026 as July to mid-September rain fell from 304 mm to 6 mm.</p>
  <p><b>Conclusions</b>A photo is a screening tool. Predicting needs from weather and sensors is where automation earns its keep, and tiers let a grower add the next step only when the last one has paid. Evidence is strong for FAO-56 water budgeting, moderate for closed-loop fertigation, and limited for image diagnosis in the field and for full autonomy.</p>
  <p class="kw"><b>Keywords</b>plant phenotyping · colour indices · evapotranspiration · controlled environment agriculture · Sentinel-2 · irrigation</p>
</section>

<h2>1. Introduction</h2>
<p>A grower makes the same decisions every day: whether to water, how much to feed, when to vent, what to look at first. Visible symptoms come late. By the time a leaf yellows, the cause has usually been at work for days. Two technologies claim to close the gap. Image models promise to read a plant from a photo; automation promises to act on sensor data without waiting for symptoms.</p>
<p>Both claims need checking. Image classifiers trained on curated photos score close to perfectly on their own test sets, but those scores say little about a phone photo in a greenhouse. Automation is often sold as an integrated package, which makes it hard for a grower to see which decision is being taken, on what evidence, and whether it pays. This study asks four questions:</p>
<ol>
  <li><b>RQ1.</b> How well does image-based plant diagnosis work outside the lab, and what can a photo reliably tell a grower?</li>
  <li><b>RQ2.</b> Which needs can be predicted from weather and sensor data before stress is visible, and with which methods?</li>
  <li><b>RQ3.</b> How should automation be staged so that each tier adds a decision the grower can verify?</li>
  <li><b>RQ4.</b> What do open satellite and weather data show for real fields in 2026, and how do the results connect to the tiers?</li>
</ol>
<p>The study comes with working tools. The Hortus research pages run the analyser and the needs predictor in the browser, so a reader can test both claims on their own plants.</p>

<h2>2. Background</h2>
<h3>2.1 Image diagnosis outside the lab</h3>
<p>The PlantVillage collection released more than 50,000 expert-curated leaf images of healthy and diseased crops (Hughes &amp; Salathé, 2015). A deep network trained on it identified 14 crops and 26 diseases with 99.35% accuracy on held-out images, but on photos from other sources, taken under different conditions, accuracy fell to 31.4%, against 2.6% for random choice (Mohanty et al., 2016). Part of the lab score is a shortcut: a model given only eight background pixels from each PlantVillage image predicted the label with 49.0% accuracy (Noyan, 2022). Singh et al. (2020) built PlantDoc, 2,598 images gathered outside the lab, because such data were missing; training on it raised classification accuracy on non-lab images by up to 31%. General multimodal models do not escape the problem. On PlantVillage apple leaves, GPT-4o reached 98.12% after fine-tuning but 56.87% zero-shot, and 69.37% zero-shot on maize (Roumeliotis et al., 2025), and those were still lab images.</p>
<h3>2.2 What colour can measure</h3>
<p>Colour indices cannot name a disease, but they measure what is there. Excess Green (2g − r − b on chromatic coordinates) separates living plants from soil and residue in sun and shade (Woebbecke et al., 1995); Otsu’s (1979) method picks the threshold from the image itself. The Visible Atmospherically Resistant Index, VARI = (G − R)/(G + R − B), tracks green vegetation fraction from visible bands alone (Gitelson et al., 2002). Together they give plant cover, greenness and the share of tissue that has turned yellow or brown: screening signals, not diagnoses.</p>
<h3>2.3 Predicting needs</h3>
<p>Water demand is the best-established prediction in agronomy. FAO-56 computes a reference evapotranspiration (ET₀) from weather with the Penman–Monteith equation and multiplies it by a crop coefficient (Kc) for the crop and stage (Allen et al., 1998). Where only temperature is known, Hargreaves and Samani’s (1985) equation gives a cruder estimate. Crop coefficients can be scaled from satellite vegetation indices (Kamble et al., 2013). Light is budgeted as the daily light integral (DLI), the photosynthetic photons received per square metre per day (Faust &amp; Logan, 2018); Michigan State University guidance suggests at least 12 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ for leafy greens and herbs and 15 for fruiting vegetables in controlled environments (Runkle, n.d.). Disease risk can be forecast from weather: the Hutton criteria, Great Britain’s warning system for potato late blight since 2017, flag two consecutive days each with a minimum of 10 °C or more and at least six hours of relative humidity at 90% or more (Dancey et al., 2017).</p>
<h3>2.4 Autonomy</h3>
<p>In the first Autonomous Greenhouse Challenge, five teams grew cucumbers remotely for about four months, each controlling a 96 m² compartment with its own algorithms, against a compartment run manually by commercial growers. The organisers concluded that, in general, AI performed well in controlling a greenhouse (Hemming et al., 2019); the winning team produced more cucumbers than the growers’ reference (VentureBeat, n.d.). That is one challenge, one crop and one season.</p>

<h2>3. Methods</h2>
<h3>3.1 The analyser</h3>
<p>The analyser runs in the browser; a photo leaves the page only if the user asks for a second opinion. Images are scaled so that the longer side is at most 640 pixels. For each pixel we compute Excess Green on chromatic coordinates, VARI, and hue, saturation and value. The plant mask is every pixel whose Excess Green exceeds an Otsu threshold (160 bins over −0.5 to 1.0, bounded between 0.03 and 0.20) and whose value exceeds 0.12. Inside the mask, pixels with hue between 40° and 75° and saturation above 0.35 count as yellowing; the rest count as healthy. Non-plant pixels count only if the plant encloses them: holes that do not reach the image edge and cover at most 2% of the frame, plus notches closed by a morphological closing of radius 0.8% of the longer side. Among those, hue 38°–75° with value above 0.35 counts as yellowing and hue 8°–38° with value below 0.75 as browning (saturation above 0.22 for both). The screening index (0–100) is the healthy share, less 0.6 times the browning share, plus up to ±5 points for mean greenness (VARI). Figure 1 shows the pipeline.</p>
<p>The optional second opinion sends the photo and the measured shares to Claude (Anthropic) with fixed rules: describe only what is visible, give causes as possibilities with a likelihood, prefer checks a grower can do (leaf undersides, roots, the pattern on old versus new leaves, a soil or feed test) before treatments, and never name pesticide products or rates. The answer comes back as structured fields that the report prints verbatim.</p>
<figure class="p-fig" data-fig="vflow"><figcaption><b>Figure 1.</b> The analyser, step by step.</figcaption></figure>
<h3>3.2 The needs predictor</h3>
<p>Daily ET₀ follows FAO-56 Penman–Monteith from maximum and minimum temperature, relative humidity, wind at 2 m, solar radiation, latitude, elevation and day of year (Allen et al., 1998). Crop water use is ET₀ × Kc, with Kc from FAO-56 Table 12 for the initial, mid-season and late stages and their mean for development. Litres per plant are crop water use times the area per plant; the interval between waterings is the usable soil water (an input) divided by daily use. Under lights, DLI = PPFD × hours × 0.0036; outdoors we assume 48% of shortwave radiation is photosynthetically active at 4.57 µmol per joule, so DLI ≈ 2.19 × solar radiation in MJ m⁻² d⁻¹. Vapour pressure deficit uses the FAO-56 vapour pressure equations. Growing degree-days use a base of 10 °C, capped at 30 °C for maize. Late blight uses the Hutton criteria; feed pH and EC are compared with a working window of pH 5.5–6.5 and with the grower’s own recipe (±0.3 mS cm⁻¹); CO₂ readings are checked against the US occupational limit of 5,000 ppm as an eight-hour average (29 CFR 1910.1000). Indoor and greenhouse ET₀ use simplified radiation terms and should be calibrated.</p>
<h3>3.3 Tiers</h3>
<p>We staged automation by what the system senses, decides and does, and mapped each tier to Hortus controllers using their published specifications (Table 2). A tier is complete only when its decisions can be checked by the grower: a reading, a rule and an action that can be overridden.</p>
<h3>3.4 Three fields from orbit</h3>
<p>We read Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface reflectance (Collection 1, 10 m) from the Earth Search archive on AWS for three 6 × 6 km windows: centre-pivot farms near 38.06° N, 101.09° W in western Kansas (tile 14SLH, March–October 2026); pivots near 13.84° S, 29.24° E at Mkushi, Zambia (35LQE, January–October 2026); and lowland rice land near 6.52° S, 108.15° E in West Java (48MZT, May–October 2025 and 2026). We kept scenes with at most 50–70% cloud over the tile, masked every pixel not classed as vegetation, bare soil or water by the scene classification layer, and dropped scenes with less than 60% valid pixels in the window. Reflectance is DN × 0.0001 − 0.1. We computed NDVI (B08, B04), NDRE (B8A, B05) and NDMI (B8A, B11).</p>
<p>In Kansas we interpolated each pixel’s NDVI to 10-day steps from 11 March to 7 October using the 34 nearly cloud-free scenes, clustered the curves with k-means (k = 5) and named the clusters from their shape. In Mkushi we classed as irrigated every pixel whose median NDVI over July and August exceeded 0.6. In West Java we took each pixel’s highest NDVI across the July–September scenes of each year and counted the share above 0.5.</p>
<p>Daily weather came from NASA POWER on AWS: MERRA-2 temperature, dew point, wind and pressure; FLASHFlux shortwave radiation; and IMERG precipitation (MERRA-2 where IMERG was missing). For each cropped class we set the season from the class NDVI curve (from 15 days before NDVI first reaches 0.30 to the last day at or above it), scaled Kc linearly from 0.30 at NDVI 0.15 to the mid-season FAO-56 value at NDVI 0.85 (maize 1.20, wheat 1.15, other summer crops 1.10), and computed crop water use with both Penman–Monteith and Hargreaves ET₀. Net irrigation need is the sum over 10-day blocks of crop water use minus 80% of rain, where positive. The range between the two ET₀ methods is reported as the uncertainty.</p>
<h3>3.5 Evidence labels</h3>
<p>As in Study 01: <span class="ev"><b>Strong</b> evidence</span> where results are consistent across many studies, sites or seasons; <span class="ev"><b>Moderate</b></span> where several studies or one solid dataset agree; <span class="ev"><b>Limited</b></span> where results rest on few studies, lab conditions or a single season.</p>

<h2>4. Results</h2>
<h3>4.1 What a photo can tell</h3>
<p>The evidence is consistent: high accuracy on curated images, large losses on photos from elsewhere, and shortcuts that inflate lab scores (Table 1). No published result we found shows a general model diagnosing diseases reliably from arbitrary phone photos in the field. <span class="ev"><b>Moderate</b> evidence</span> that field accuracy falls far below lab accuracy; <span class="ev"><b>Limited</b></span> for any specific field accuracy.</p>
<div class="p-table" data-table="vision-evidence"><p class="cap"><b>Table 1.</b> Image-based plant diagnosis: lab results against results elsewhere.</p></div>
<p>On three synthetic test images the analyser behaves as designed (Figure 2). The healthy plant scores 99.6% healthy tissue and a screening index of 100; the plant with leaf spots scores 82.2% healthy, 9.0% yellowing (the halos) and 8.8% browning (the spots), index 81; the plant with yellowing patches scores 46.0% healthy and 52.6% yellowing, index 45. Before the enclosed-region rule, soil at the leaf edges was read as browning and the healthy plant scored only 77% healthy; restricting off-mask tissue to regions the plant encloses removed that error. We have not yet validated the analyser on labelled field photos. <span class="ev"><b>Limited</b> evidence</span></p>
<figure class="p-fig" data-fig="samples"><figcaption><b>Figure 2.</b> The analyser on three synthetic images: photo (top) and stress map (bottom). Stress map: yellowing white, browning grey, healthy green dimmed.</figcaption></figure>

<h3>4.2 Predicting needs</h3>
<p>For irrigated maize at mid-season under the June–August 2026 means for the Kansas window (maximum 36.5 °C, minimum 21.4 °C, 47% relative humidity, wind 3.5 m s⁻¹, 26.5 MJ m⁻² d⁻¹), the model gives ET₀ 8.1 mm d⁻¹ and crop water use 9.7 mm d⁻¹, or 1.26 L per plant per day at 0.13 m² per plant; the vapour pressure deficit is 2.3 kPa and the daily maximum crosses the 35 °C heat flag. For a mid-season greenhouse tomato under the example readings (27/17 °C, 70% relative humidity, 12 MJ m⁻² d⁻¹ inside), use is 2.9 mm d⁻¹, 1.4 L per plant at 0.5 m², light 26 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ (above the 15 minimum), and one of the two Hutton days is met. These are estimates whose method is mature; the inputs, especially wind and radiation inside a greenhouse, decide their accuracy. <span class="ev"><b>Strong</b> evidence</span> for the method; <span class="ev"><b>Moderate</b></span> for greenhouse and indoor inputs</p>

<h3>4.3 Five tiers</h3>
<p>The tiers add one kind of decision at a time (Table 2; Figure 3). Tier 0 needs only a phone and screens for stress. Tier 1 senses the root zone and the air and acts on thresholds. Tier 2 closes control loops on feed, pH, climate and CO₂. Tier 3 predicts: it moves set points with the weather forecast and the crop’s stage, and warns before stress shows. Tier 4 optimises: it trades yield against energy, water and CO₂, with people auditing the system. Each tier keeps the one below working on its own, so a network or cloud failure degrades the system by one step instead of stopping it.</p>

<h3>4.4 Kansas: five crop calendars and a hot summer</h3>
<p>NDVI curves separated five crop calendars in the 36 km² Kansas window (Figures 4 and 5): irrigated maize, peaking at NDVI 0.87 in late July (45.7% of the window); winter wheat followed by a second summer crop (16.3%); winter wheat followed by fallow (8.8%); a late-sown summer crop peaking in mid-August (12.0%); and dryland, fallow and field corners (17.1%). The summer was hot: the June–August mean daily maximum was 36.5 °C, 2.9 °C above the 1991–2020 normal and level with 2012 (36.4 °C); 75 days from May to September reached 35 °C.</p>
<p>From 27 May to 1 October the irrigated maize used an estimated 735 mm (Hargreaves) to 936 mm (Penman–Monteith) against 436 mm of rain, for a net irrigation need of 520–714 mm (Figure 6). The Penman–Monteith total sits high; reanalysis wind and humidity on a half-degree grid may overstate demand at field scale, and the budget carries no soil-water storage from one 10-day block to the next. The double-cropped fields needed more over their two crops (761–1,038 mm). <span class="ev"><b>Moderate</b> evidence</span></p>
<figure class="p-fig" data-fig="kmap"><figcaption><b>Figure 4.</b> Kansas window at peak canopy (NDVI, 30 July 2026, 6 × 6 km). Bright dots are high NDVI; grey dots are bare or senescent ground.</figcaption></figure>

<h3>4.5 Mkushi: water only from the pivot</h3>
<p>At Mkushi, pivots covered 25.6% of the window and stood out against the land around them through the dry season: pivot NDVI peaked at 0.87, while the rest of the window dried down to 0.22 (Figure 7). From 20 April to 24 September the winter wheat used an estimated 536–646 mm while 2.6 mm of rain fell, so practically all of it came from irrigation (534–644 mm). Daily demand peaked at 6.6 mm. <span class="ev"><b>Moderate</b> evidence</span></p>
<figure class="p-fig" data-fig="mk"><figcaption><b>Figure 7.</b> NDVI of the irrigated pivots and of everything else in the Mkushi window, 2026.</figcaption></figure>

<h3>4.6 West Java: the drought, seen from orbit</h3>
<p>In the West Java window, 91.5% of the land reached an NDVI of 0.5 at some point in July–September 2025, against 62.4% in 2026; the median of each pixel’s seasonal maximum fell from 0.76 to 0.58 (Figure 8). Rain from 1 July to 14 September fell from 304 mm in 2025 to 6 mm in 2026. The images show large bare and dry patches where rice stood a year earlier. This is the El Niño drought measured in Study 01 (Java’s July–September rain −85%), seen field by field. <span class="ev"><b>Moderate</b> evidence</span> (two seasons, one window)</p>

<h2>5. Discussion</h2>
<p><b>RQ1.</b> A photo reliably tells a grower where to look: how much of the plant is green, how much has yellowed or browned, and whether that share is growing between photos of the same plant. It does not reliably tell why. Yellowing can come from nitrogen shortage, waterlogging, cold, age or a virus; the colour is the same. The published gap between lab and field accuracy, and the shortcut that background pixels offer, argue for a screening tool with stated limits rather than a diagnosis button. A multimodal model is useful as a second opinion that names checks, not as an oracle.</p>
<p><b>RQ2.</b> Water, light, heat and some disease risks can be predicted from weather before a plant shows stress, with methods decades old and well tested. Kansas in 2026 shows the stakes: at 9–10 mm a day in July, a maize field draws down 60–70 mm of soil water a week, and irrigation has to keep pace through flowering. The prediction is only as good as its inputs, which is why tier 3 sits on top of the sensors of tiers 1 and 2: on-site temperature, humidity and soil moisture correct the reanalysis.</p>
<p><b>RQ3.</b> Tiers make the economics legible. Moisture-triggered watering (tier 1) replaces watering by the calendar with watering by need; closed-loop feed and pH control (tier 2) is widely used in hydroponics, and its weak point is the probes, which drift and need regular calibration; prediction (tier 3) moves the set points before the weather turns; autonomy (tier 4) remains a research frontier with one strong demonstration. A grower can stop at any tier and still have a working system.</p>
<p><b>RQ4.</b> Open satellite and weather data already show what each field needed this year, at 10 m, within days. The same pipeline that separates crop calendars in Kansas and finds dry rice land in Java can set Kc and irrigation targets for a tier-3 controller, or flag a field whose canopy falls behind its class.</p>
<h3>Limitations</h3>
<ul>
  <li><b>No field validation of the analyser.</b> The thresholds are set from the literature and tested on synthetic images only.</li>
  <li><b>Water budgets</b> use reanalysis weather, a linear NDVI–Kc scaling with assumed end points, a fixed 80% rain efficiency and no soil storage; the two ET₀ methods differ by about a quarter.</li>
  <li><b>Crop classes</b> are unsupervised and named from curve shape; we did not check them against field records.</li>
  <li><b>Thresholds for pH, EC and VPD</b> are practitioner working ranges, not crop-specific targets.</li>
  <li><b>Conflict of interest.</b> Hortus sells the tier 1–2 controllers named here; the tier logic does not depend on them.</li>
</ul>

<h2>6. Recommendations</h2>
<ul>
  <li><b>Home and small growers:</b> start at tier 0–1. Photograph the same plant from the same spot weekly and watch the trend; add a moisture-triggered pump before anything else. <span class="ev"><b>Moderate</b></span></li>
  <li><b>Greenhouse and hydroponic growers:</b> close the feed and pH loops (tier 2) and calibrate probes on a schedule; drift is the default. <span class="ev"><b>Moderate</b></span></li>
  <li><b>Field growers with irrigation:</b> budget water with ET₀ × Kc from local weather and check it against a soil probe; in a summer like Kansas 2026, plan for 9–10 mm a day at peak. <span class="ev"><b>Strong</b></span></li>
  <li><b>Everyone:</b> treat an image diagnosis as a reason to look closer, never as a reason to spray. <span class="ev"><b>Strong</b></span></li>
</ul>

<h2>7. Conclusion</h2>
<p>A photo is the cheapest sensor on a farm and the easiest to over-trust. Used as a screen, it tells a grower where to look; combined with weather, sensors and models, it becomes part of a system that predicts what a plant needs before the plant shows it. Built in tiers, that system can grow with the grower, one checked decision at a time.</p>
